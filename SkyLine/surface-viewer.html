GOOD QUESTIONS TO START WITH

A little context
goes a long way.

New to the Moon? You’re in the right place.

Why focus on the south pole?

The Sun stays close to the horizon near the pole, so terrain makes a big difference to light and shadow. Some crater interiors remain in permanent shadow. Nearby areas can receive very different amounts of sunlight.

Is this a live view of the Moon?

No. The maps combine recorded orbital images. Surface View reconstructs the terrain using height data and those photos. It is not a live feed or a Street View photograph.

What do “rim”, “ridge”, and “relay” mean?

A rim is the raised edge of a crater. A ridge is a long area of high ground. A relay satellite passes messages between places that cannot communicate directly.

Can Skyline tell us where it is safe to land?

This prototype helps explain the questions and explore the landscape. It does not certify a landing site or a safe route. Actual mission planning needs much more detailed data, validated calculations, and engineering checks.

Δvavailable = g0 Isp ln(mwet / mfinal), keeping your reserve. Demand is an ideal impulsive circular-to-surface transfer and full tangential braking, plus your allowance. It is NOT a feasible powered-descent trajectory. Gravity/steering losses, throttle limits, navigation, divert and terminal landing dynamics are not solved. T/W above 1 only permits ideal hover; it does not prove safe landing.

05 · Terrain scale & landing dispersion
Regional slope · 80 m grid—
Gaussian 95% ellipse area—

The ellipse assumes independent Gaussian axes and uses √χ²(2, 0.95) = 2.448. It is a position-containment model, NOT a probability of safe touchdown. The slope is a central-difference estimate over 160 m baselines, not lander-footpad hazard clearance. No Monte Carlo or hazard map is applied.

WHY EARTH INTUITION CAN MISLEAD

Same questions. Very different conditions.

On Earth

The atmosphere bends light near the horizon. We are familiar with a daily sunrise and sunset. A distant hill can still block the view.

Near the Moon’s south pole

There is no substantial atmosphere to provide Earth-like refraction. The Sun moves slowly and stays low. The spherical Moon and local terrain both shape the horizon. Earth also shifts in the sky because of libration; it is not a fixed marker.

How do Satellite and Surface View work together?

Satellite View shows recorded LROC photographs. Surface View reconstructs the same landscape using NASA LOLA elevations and the georeferenced photo texture; it is not an actual ground-level photo or Street View panorama. The three 32 km study patches limit where you can move. Click-to-move follows the elevation surface but does not check slopes, hazards or traversability. The camera follows the sampled mesh at your chosen height: 3 m, 80 m or 300 m. The default 80 m view makes regional relief easier to inspect at the available resolution. Photo shadows remain fixed; they do not follow the time slider. Dark or missing detail is not invented. NASA-data analysis is computed at the fixed study centers, independently of the camera. Data, assumptions & what still needs validation

JPL Horizons airless, topocentric Sun/Earth center azimuth and elevation, MEAN_ME / DE441. The observer is 2 m above the DEM elevation at each fixed study center. Lunar orientation and orbital variation come from Horizons, not a repeated sine wave.

The terrain mask uses the NASA 80 m LOLA DEM, 0.5° azimuth steps and spherical rays out to 250 km. Lunar radius: 1,737.4 km. No atmospheric refraction. Near rays use 40 m sampling, far rays 160 m; this does not create finer terrain detail. The DEM is MOON_ME / DE421; its residual registration to Horizons’ frame has not been independently validated.

Transition times are brackets between hourly samples. Narrow blockers, partial Sun/Earth discs, eclipse effects, slope safety, panel orientation, power storage, radio link budgets and uncertainty propagation are not computed. The surface camera does not move this analysis observer. Frame alignment, horizon convergence and reference-tool agreement still require independent validation before operational use.

  • Sun/Earth azimuth and elevation from precomputed Horizons data.

PARTIAL / REQUIRES VALIDATION

Scientific and operational readiness

  • Ephemerides cover 1 Nov 2026–1 Feb 2027 at three fixed centers, not arbitrary places or dates.
  • Lunar motion is in Horizons, but DEM/ephemeris frame registration and horizon convergence need independent checks.
  • No Sun/Earth disc fractions, eclipse model, energy budget, radio link budget or safe landing/rover route calculation.
  • 80 m terrain and hourly samples miss fine hazards and short events; uncertainty is not propagated.
  • Saved CSVs support handoff; live multi-user coordination and real relay orbit propagation are not implemented.
